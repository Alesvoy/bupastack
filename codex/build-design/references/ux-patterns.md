# UX patterns

Pick the patterns that fit the screen; skip the rest. A pattern applied where it doesn't belong reads as manipulation or clutter. Each entry says what it is, where it fits, how to apply it in product UI, and when not to use it.

## Most product screens

- **Von Restorff effect:** one element differs visually so it's noticed first. Apply it to the single primary action or the one number that matters. Not to several things at once; three highlighted items means none is.
- **Choice overload (Hick's law):** fewer options decide faster. Show the few common choices, group the rest, and add search or filters once a list passes about ten items. Don't hide options people need every day.
- **Miller's law:** people hold about five to nine chunks. Group navigation, form fields, and metrics into small labeled sets. It's a guide for chunking, not a hard count.
- **Fitts's law:** big, close targets are faster. Make the primary action large and near where the eye and pointer already are (end of a form, bottom of a mobile screen). Keep destructive actions small and away from it.
- **Smart defaults:** pre-fill the most likely choice (today's date, the last project used, the usual quantity). Never pre-select anything that costs money, shares data, or can't be undone.
- **Aesthetic-usability effect:** polished interfaces feel easier and earn patience. Invest in alignment, spacing, and type on the main screen. It doesn't excuse a broken flow.
- **Zeigarnik effect:** unfinished tasks stay on the mind. Use quiet cues for real unfinished work: a badge count of items to review, a "stale" marker, a step left. Never invent unfinished tasks to pull users back.

## Multi-step flows (uploads, bookings, checkout, onboarding)

- **Goal gradient effect:** people speed up as the finish gets closer. Show real progress through the steps ("Step 2 of 3", a filled step bar). Don't fake a head start in a work tool; count only steps the user actually did.
- **Peak-end rule:** people remember the best moment and the end. Make the finish clear and satisfying: a confirmation that says what happened and what's next. Keep it proportional; no confetti for routine work.
- **Labor illusion:** visible work builds trust in slow or sensitive steps. Show real progress during validation or processing ("Checking 24 rows…"). Never add fake delay.

## Marketing, sign-up, and pricing surfaces only

These drive conversion. In internal tools and everyday product screens they come across as manipulation; skip them there.

- **Social proof:** real usage numbers, reviews, or customer logos near the decision point. Only real data.
- **Reciprocity:** give real value first (a free partial report or preview) before asking for sign-up.
- **IKEA / endowment effect:** let people set something up before creating an account, so they value what they built.
- **Loss aversion:** frame what the user keeps or loses. Use honestly, such as "your draft isn't saved yet", never with fake urgency.
- **Contrast effect:** the first number shown anchors the next. On pricing, show the full plan before add-ons. Never invent a crossed-out price.
